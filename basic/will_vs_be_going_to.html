01

Core Idea

The Key Difference

Both refer to the future — but Will is typically spontaneous or general, while Be Going To has a connection to the present: a prior decision or visible evidence.

Will

Decision made at the moment of speaking — not planned before.

General prediction based on personal opinion, not on something you can see right now.

Offers, promises, threats.

Be Going To

Decision or intention already made before the moment of speaking.

Prediction based on present evidence — something you can see or feel right now.

Something that is about to happen any second.

The bottom line: Ask yourself — did I just decide this, or did I decide it before? If you decided it just now → Will. If you decided it earlier → Be Going To.
Both forms are so common in everyday English that native speakers mix them regularly. Even if you misuse them, a native speaker will understand you without any problem.
That said, there are specific situations where only one is correct — and those are the cases worth learning. The rest of this guide focuses on exactly those situations.
Watch out for predictions: Both will and be going to can express predictions — this is the area of greatest overlap. The real difference shows up with decisions (spontaneous vs prior) and evidence-based predictions.
02

Will Only

When Only Will Works

Three situations where Be Going To cannot replace Will.

Will only — Case 1
Rapid / Spontaneous Decision
You decide to do something at that exact moment, not before. There was no prior plan.
"The phone is ringing." → "I'll get it!"
"I'll buy one for you too." (just decided)
Will only — Case 2
Offers, Promises & Threats
When you offer help, make a promise, or issue a warning in response to the situation.
"I'll give you a discount if you buy now."
"I promise I will behave next time."
"I'll take you to the movies if you'd like."
Will only — Case 3
Refusal (Won't)
When someone or something refuses or is unwilling to do something — won't expresses this.
"I told him to take out the trash but he won't do it."
"My car won't start."
  • 01"I'll buy one for you too." will only — spontaneous decision at that moment
  • 02"I think I'll try one of those." will only — just decided
  • 03"I'll give you a discount if you buy it now." will only — offer
  • 04"I promise I will behave next time." will only — promise
  • 05"My kids won't listen to anything I say." will only — refusal
  • 06"I told him to clean his room but he won't do it." will only — refusal
03

Be Going To Only

When Only Going To Works

Three situations where Will cannot replace Be Going To.

Going To only — Case 1
Prior Plan or Intention
The decision was made before the moment of speaking. The plan already exists.
"Are you busy this evening?" → "Yes, I'm going to the movies." (planned earlier)
"I'm going to accept the job offer." (already decided)
Going To only — Case 2
Evidence-Based Prediction
Something is clearly about to happen based on what you can see, hear, or feel right now.
"I think it is going to rain — I just felt a drop."
"I don't feel well. I think I'm going to throw up."
Going To only — Case 3
Imminent Action
Something is about to happen any second — the action is already in motion.
"Get back! The bomb is going to explode!"
"Watch out — you're going to fall!"
  • 01"They're going to retire to the beach — they've already bought a little beach house." going to only
  • 02"I'm going to accept the job offer." going to only — already decided
  • 03"I think it is going to rain — I just felt a drop." going to only — present evidence
  • 04"I don't feel well. I think I'm going to throw up." going to only — body sensation = evidence
  • 05"Get back! The bomb is going to explode!" going to only — imminent
  • 06"Watch out — you're going to drop that!" going to only — imminent, visible evidence
04

Overlap

When Both Work

For general predictions and open-ended future scenarios, will and be going to are interchangeable — the meaning is the same.

General predictions (opinions about the future, not tied to present evidence) work equally well with both forms:
Will

"The weather report says it will rain tomorrow." ✓

"My team will not win the league this season." ✓

"I think it will be a great party." ✓

Be Going To

"The weather report says it is going to rain tomorrow." ✓

"My team is not going to win the league." ✓

"I think it is going to be a great party." ✓

Open-ended future (no prior plan): If you have not made any plans yet, both forms are possible. The difference is minimal.
"I will probably watch TV." = "I'm probably going to watch TV." ✓
  • ✓"It will rain tomorrow." = "It is going to rain tomorrow." both OK
  • ✓"I think you will find it interesting." = "I think you're going to find it interesting." both OK
  • ✓"I'll probably watch TV." = "I'm probably going to watch TV." both OK — no prior plan
  • ✓"She will be a great teacher." = "She's going to be a great teacher." both OK — general opinion

05

Third Option

Present Continuous for the Future

There is a third way to talk about the future: the Present Continuous — used for fixed, confirmed arrangements with another person.

The Present Continuous is used for arrangements — events that are confirmed with another person and where some preparation has already happened. More than one person knows about the event.
Going To (intention)

"I'm going to call Jim."
→ my own plan, not confirmed with Jim yet

Present Continuous (arrangement)

"I'm meeting Jim at the airport."
→ Jim and I have discussed this — it's confirmed

Key signal: If the other person is already aware of the arrangement — use Present Continuous. If it's just your own intention or plan → use Be Going To.
  • 01"I'm meeting Jim at the airport." — Jim and I have discussed this
  • 02"I'm leaving tomorrow." — ticket already bought, confirmed
  • 03"We're having a staff meeting next Monday." — all staff have been told
  • 04"My brother is playing football with his friends tonight." — arranged with his friends
  • 05"Are you having a party for your birthday?" — asking about a confirmed arrangement
  • 06"They aren't leaving until the end of next year." — confirmed, fixed arrangement
Simple Present ≠ Present Continuous for the future. The Simple Present is used when a future event is part of a timetable or programme.
Present Continuous

"We're having a staff meeting next Monday."
→ just that once, arranged between the team

Simple Present

"We have a staff meeting every Monday."
→ scheduled routine, part of a regular timetable
